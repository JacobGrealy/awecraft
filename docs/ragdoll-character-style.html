Procedural primitive characters with a seam-blended skin

AC-0090 · design essay prototype built and measured
AweCraft · Three.js web prototype · 2026-09-19 · code: prototypes/ac-0090-ragdoll/ · evidence: tasks/AC-0090/AC-0090-results.html

The question this ticket asks is narrow and visual: can a character assembled from obvious primitives — capsules, blobs, rounded boxes — be made to read as ONE seamless body, cheaply enough for a phone, with toon shading, and driven entirely by procedural animation for two-legged, any-legged, legless and flying body plans?

Short answer: yes, and the seam is best solved with geometry and skin weights rather than with a shader. The surprising part of the work was not the blending at all — it was that six separate defects in the rig and the weight bake produced a creature that looked entirely plausible in a still frame while being structurally broken. Every one of them was caught by a number, never by looking. That is the finding worth carrying into the game, and it is the reason this essay spends as much space on verification as on technique.

1. Verdict up front

QuestionAnswerEvidence
Does the technique produce one seamless body? Yes. A limb is a single tapered tube that spans every joint of its chain, so a joint is a continuous surface rather than two cylinders butting together. The alternative — polygonising an implicit union — is implemented too and is more seamless, at a bake cost. §3, §4
Is it mobile-cheap? Yes for the runtime; the cost is in the bake. One skinned mesh, one draw call, one shader program, 4,464–7,420 triangles, no textures, no screen-space passes. §10
Does the deformation hold together? Yes. Max edge stretch over a walk cycle is 1.09–1.86× across the five body plans, where a rigid body is 1.00×. An earlier revision of this page reported 2.4–10.1× as an honest residual; it was not a property of the weights at all but a forward-kinematics bug that collapsed the whole character (defect 1 below). §5, §9, §10
Is the procedural locomotion good enough? Yes for a prototype, with one honest gap: knees/elbows animate without a solver, and foot lock is right by construction in the gait frame — but there is no general IK chain and no world-space foot planting. §8
Is it worth porting into the Godot game? The technique, yes. This code, no. What transfers is the envelope-plus-arc-length-weight recipe and the numeric gates; the JS is a research artifact. §12

2. The problem, stated precisely

A "ragdoll-like" character made of primitives fails in a specific, recognisable way. Each part is individually correct, and the assembly reads as a pile of parts:

  1. The intersection crease. Where an arm capsule enters a torso blob, the two surfaces cross. The visible result is a hard circular seam with a crease in the shading — the single strongest "assembled from parts" cue.
  2. The butt joint. Two cylinders placed end to end at an elbow show a hard rim even when they overlap, because the silhouette diameter jumps.
  3. The tear. Once the parts are weighted to a rig, a vertex near a joint is claimed by two bones. If the claim is wrong, the limb separates when it bends — sometimes by an enormous factor, while the rest pose looks perfect.

The prototype's job was to attack all three, and to be honest about which are solved and which are reduced.

3. The seam technique, and the four that were rejected

Chosen: one tapered tube envelope per limb chain, in a smooth-min radius union

A limb is not built as one primitive per bone. It is built as a single tapered tube that spans the whole chain: a Catmull-Rom centreline through the limb's joints, with a radius profile r(t) interpolated between the joints' radii. Shoulder→elbow→wrist is one tube with three control points, not two capsules.

That single decision removes problems (1) and (2) for the limb itself: there is no intersection in the middle of a limb, because there is only one surface. The enlargement at each joint — the "joint ball" that keeps the elbow from pinching when it bends — is not a separate sphere in the fast tier; it is a local widening of the same tube's radius profile. One surface, no seam, and the silhouette flows through the joint.

Where the tube meets the torso, the overlap is resolved by profile shape rather than by a blend: a limb's first control point is placed inside the torso volume and given a radius that keeps the tube's surface within the torso's silhouette across the whole overlap. Because the torso surface is the outer envelope, the buried intersection is never drawn, and the visible transition is where the tube naturally emerges — a smooth taper, not a crease.

Rejected A: implicit surface, polygonised (implemented anyway, as tier 3)

Turn every primitive into a signed distance field, combine with a smooth minimum so intersections melt, then polygonise the result. This is the textbook-correct answer to "one seamless body": the output has no separate surfaces left, so there is nothing to blend. It is implemented here (src/metaball.js, ~430 lines with a bucket grid and surface nets) and it works. Why it is not the default: it is a CPU bake at generation time — tens to hundreds of milliseconds per character at usable resolution — and it produces a mesh whose topology the caller cannot predict or budget. On a phone the runtime is fine but the first-frame cost and the memory spike are not. It also needs its skin weights transferred from the fast tier, which is a real dependency (§5).

Rejected B: screen-space or depth-based blur over the intersection

Draw the parts, blur the seam. Rejected on cost and on look: it needs a full-screen pass (the one thing a mobile fill-rate budget cannot absorb), it needs depth and/or normal buffers, and the result reads as out-of-focus rather than as one body. Blur is not the same visual as a continuous surface.

Rejected C: per-vertex SDF displacement in the vertex shader

Keep the primitive meshes but, in the shader, push each vertex onto the smooth-min of all primitives' fields. Genuinely elegant, and rejected for two concrete reasons: every primitive's parameters must be available in the vertex shader (a uniform array or texture — a per-frame upload and a shader the compiler cannot unroll), and the vertices must be dense enough to resolve the bent surface, which defeats the point of low-poly primitives.

Rejected D: nothing — accept the seams and lean into "robot"

Legitimate, and explicitly not what the ticket asked for. The ticket's requirement is that the parts read as one organic body, so the prototype has to demonstrate that.

The load-bearing idea. A primitive body can be made seamless without remeshing and without a screen-space pass if the primitives are shaped to overlap smoothly to begin with. The tube envelope is that shaping: it converts a chain of primitives into one surface whose radius varies smoothly, and smooth variation is what the eye reads as "one body".

4. The quality ladder: three tiers from one body plan

Rather than argue about which approach is best, the prototype exposes all three on the same body plan so the difference is a button, not a claim:

TierWhat it isSeamCost
hard The raw primitives with per-primitive flat shading and no joint blending — the "before" arm. Visible everywhere, by construction Same geometry as smooth
smooth The tube envelopes with arc-length-weighted joints and smooth normals. This is the mobile default. Gone within a limb; the torso transition is a smooth emergence Same geometry — the blending is baked, not computed
remesh Smooth-min distance field, polygonised with surface nets and projected onto the true surface. Gone, structurally — there is one surface CPU bake + unpredictable triangle count

hard and smooth are the same vertex buffer — the difference is which normal attribute the shader reads and whether the baked weights are applied. That makes the A/B honest: no geometry change, no trickery.

5. Deformation: why weights, not shaders, decide whether it tears

This is where the work actually was, and where the prototype's most transferable lesson lives.

A vertex near a joint is influenced by two bones. The weight transition — how the influence hands over from one bone to the next along the limb — is the single most important number in the whole system. Three separate things had to be right at once:

  1. Where the transition is centred. It must be centred on the joint. The first implementation mapped the tube's uniform curve parameter onto a joint index; because joints are not equally spaced along a curve, the window landed in the wrong place. The second implementation segmented the arc between joints — also not centred. The fix is to measure each ring's position by arc length, find the nearest joint along that arc, and blend symmetrically about it.
  2. How wide it is. Wider means a gentler gradient and less stretch, but the window cannot exceed about a quarter of the limb segment before the blend swallows the whole segment. The measured sweep (biped, worst edge stretch over a walk cycle): 1.6 radii → 22.6×, 3 → 15.4×, 5 → 10.5×, 8 → 6.5×, 12 → 9.7×. It flattens, then degrades. The shipped window is about 2.2 radii, capped at half the shorter neighbouring bone so that two adjacent joints' windows can never overlap. Letting the window exceed the bone (the hopper's knee-to-ankle is shorter than its window) turns the whole bone into a transition and is the single largest contributor to the residual stretch.
  3. What moves with what. Any lump that straddles a joint — a shoulder ball, a foot block — must share the tube's blend, not be rigid to one bone. A rigid ball overlapping a blended tube tears away from it, and because the ball is thick the tear is large. Those lumps now borrow the weights of the nearest tube vertex.

Linear blend skinning vs dual quaternions

The shader implements both, behind a uniform, so the trade is measurable rather than asserted: uDqs selects dual-quaternion skinning (blend the rotations as quaternions and the translations as their dual parts, then rebuild a rigid transform) instead of blending four matrices. DQS is provably rigid — a two-bone unit test in tools/meshcheck.mjs shows LBS compressing a 0.2-unit gap to 0.1755 where DQS holds it at exactly 0.2000 — and it costs roughly 100 extra ALU per vertex (4 matrix→quaternion decompositions plus a quaternion→matrix rebuild).

An honest negative result, and a removed claim. The shader path was implemented and then taken back out, because the offline mirror that was supposed to prove it better reproduced the LBS number instead — 1.242 vs 1.242, 10.153 vs 10.122, and so on to four decimal places. A comparison that cannot disagree is not evidence, so the number was retired rather than tuned until it passed. What survives is the one claim the unit test can support: a dual-quaternion blend of two rigid transforms is rigid — a two-bone test holds a 0.2-unit gap at exactly 0.2000 where LBS compresses it to 0.1755. Whether that beats LBS on these characters is not established, and the measured max edge stretch under LBS (2.4–10.1×) is the number a DQS implementation would have to improve on if someone revives it.

6. The procedural body generator

A character is data, not a model. One compact plan drives everything:

  • A joint graph: named joints with a parent chain, in character space (+Y up, +Z forward).
  • A list of shapes: tube (a chain of joints plus a radius per joint) and lump (a superellipsoid — round=1 is an ellipsoid, round=0 a rounded box, so torso blocks and heads come from the same primitive).
  • Locomotion wiring: which joints form each leg and arm chain, the gait name, the speed scale.

Everything downstream reads that structure: the skin weights come from the shape's own tube chain, the locomotion rig never special-cases a creature by name, and the implicit tier consumes the same shape list — so the three quality tiers describe the same animal rather than three similar ones.

Five presets exercise the space: a biped (2 legs + arms), a quadruped (4 legs, trot), a hexapod (6 legs, tripod), a hopper (0 functional legs — fused, tucked legs and a squash cycle) and a flyer (wings plus small arms plus tucked legs). Generation is deterministic in (preset, seed): the seed moves joint positions by a few percent, breathes the radii, and rotates the palette, all through a seeded PRNG, with a geometry digest asserted equal across two generations and different across two seeds.

Tessellation is adaptive from the shape's own radius (ring count and ring spacing both scale with r relative to the character's height), so a 2 cm finger does not cost what a 20 cm torso does. A quality dial scales the whole budget.

The seed is a generator, not a colour dial. An earlier revision of this prototype had the seed move every joint by 0.012–0.018 world units and every radius by ±7%. Measured across five seeds, a biped's width/height ratio moved from 0.3685 to 0.3714 — 0.8% — so every seed produced the same creature in a different palette. Fixed absolute offsets are also wrong in principle: ±0.018 is 0.9% of a 1.92-unit biped and 4.6% of a 0.39-unit flyer, so the same number meant different things.

What reads as a different creature is proportion, so that is what the seed now varies, each axis drawn independently and expressed as a ratio so it means the same thing at any scale:

  • Limb length and thickness — per limb, so a creature can come out long-legged and spindly or short-legged and stout, and differently on each side of the body. Joint chains are scaled about their root joint, so a longer leg grows away from the hip instead of sliding off the body.
  • Head size, the single biggest silhouette cue on a chunky creature.
  • Torso length along the spine, and a modest cross-section change.
  • Leg count on the many-legged preset — 4, 6 or 8. The gait needs no special-casing for this because _setupGait derives its phases from the leg count rather than assuming six: 4 legs get a trot, 6 a tripod, 8 a generic wave.
  • Stance width, plus residual jitter now scaled to the creature's own height.

Measured over eight seeds, width/height spread is 4.8% on the biped, 8.5% on the quadruped, 7.0% on the hopper and 8.6% on the flyer; head size spans 36–46% of height; the many-legged preset ranges from a 6,828-triangle quadruped to an 11,050-triangle octopod. Five offline checks now assert this, because nothing tested it before — the reason the seed could quietly collapse to a colour dial is that no gate was asking whether it generated anything.

7. Toon shading and the outline

All of it lives in one vertex/fragment program pair (src/shaders.js):

  • Banded diffuse on a wrapped lambertian (dot*0.5+0.5, so a facet facing away from the light is shaded rather than black). The band count is a uniform — 2 to 6 bands, live.
  • Rim light — one fresnel term, the cheapest way to make an untextured body read as volume.
  • Inverted-hull outline — the same program with uOutline > 0, back-face culling, and the vertex pushed along its normal. One extra draw call, no extra geometry, no post-process pass, and no second shader program to compile.
  • Debug outputs — per-primitive flat colours (the "raw parts" arm) and a blend-window view that tints exactly the vertices whose weights mix two bones. Those two views are what make the seam work visible instead of a claim.

The toon ramp is applied through the blended normals, so a band does not break along a joint — which is itself a seam test: a band that kinks at the elbow means the normals are not continuous.

8. The procedural locomotion rig

No clips, no imported animation, no retargeting. Each body plan declares a gait, and the same rig primitives (swing, bend, torso bob, squash) are driven from it.

PlanMotion
2 legsAlternating stance/swing with counter-swinging arms, two bobs per cycle, head counter-rotation so the gaze stays level.
N legsPhase offsets derived from the leg's front-to-back position: diagonal trot for 4, alternating tripods for 6, a wave gait for anything else.
0 legsA squash-and-stretch hop: wind-up crouch, launch, airborne arc, landing compression, with the fused legs agreeing with the body.
Fly + armsA flap cycle with the outer wing lagging the inner one, body bob, and a slow bank; small arms tucked and counter-swinging.
Foot lock by construction. During stance, the foot is pushed backwards along the body's own −Z by exactly one stride over the stance period, and the forward speed is derived from that stride rather than dialled in separately. The ground grid scrolls at the same derived speed, so the planted foot stays planted in world space — no IK, no fudge factor.

The phase always advances at the full gait cadence; the idle↔move blend scales the motion's amplitude only. (An earlier revision scaled the cadence by the blend, which collapsed the stride and then divided by it — see defect 4 below.)

Bone frames: why mirroring needs no special cases

Each bone's local frame is built once from the direction of its rest child: y along the bone, x = normalize(up × y), z = x × y. Mirroring a limb across x = 0 leaves both x and z unchanged, so "swing" and "bend" mean the same thing on the left and right sides. That is the whole reason the animation code has no left/right branches.

9. The defects that a still frame cannot show

This is the section worth reading before porting any of it. The prototype rendered, moved and looked like creatures through all of the following. Some were found by a number, some only after the numbers were made stricter, and two were found only by asking the browser a direct question instead of trusting a screenshot.

#DefectSymptom that exposed it
1 Forward kinematics subtracted the joint twice, collapsing the entire character. world[i] was built with a helper that already produces T(origin) · R · T(−joint) — a rest→posed point transform — and the skin matrix then multiplied by restInv = T(−joint) as well. At rest the skin matrix came out T(−joint) instead of the identity, so every vertex was dragged to its own bone's joint and the creature collapsed to a lump the size of its torso. It also explains the tearing: every bone's transform was wrong, so blended vertices were pulled in different directions. Looking at a screenshot. No gate caught it: the mesh was watertight and correctly oriented, every bone skinned geometry, the rest pose was self-consistent, the rig animated, determinism held, and 7 of 7 browser gates passed. What caught it was rendering a biped at a known camera and seeing a smooth blob with no limbs — then confirming it by reproducing the shader's transform on the CPU from the very buffers the shader reads (boneData + aBi/aBw): 1.815 units of mesh rendered 0.474 units tall. Offline, the same rig produced 1.816. Fixing it took max edge stretch from 2.4–10.1× to 1.09–1.86× — the "residual tearing" this page previously reported as inherent was entirely this bug.
2 The body mesh was never drawn at all. The character pipeline builds three materials — body, "raw primitives", and the outline hull — and only two of them were given a model matrix each frame. The body therefore rendered with the identity matrix its material was constructed with, at world origin, while its object sat 4.9 units to the left. The outline hull drew in the right place. Compounding it, the matrix it was handed was the object's own matrixWorld, so the "sync" wrote the group transform onto the very object it read from — a no-op. The page showed a faint dark silhouette and nothing else. The scene graph was correct, the projection maths was correct, the shader compiled and linked, gl.getError() was 0, and the console was silent. What found it was dumping the shader uniforms and comparing uModel against matrixWorld — [0,0,0] against [-4.91,0,0] — and then swapping in a plain MeshBasicMaterial, which drew instantly.
3 Every shape's triangles addressed the previous shape's vertices. The tube builder records its ring offsets as local indices but the index buffer is global, and the offset was only added in some of the three places that emit indices. The result: 12 vertices per tube were never referenced by any triangle, their flat normals stayed zero-length, and the "raw primitives" tier rendered as noise. "unskinned bones: lC0_r, lC1_r" — an entire leg that no animation could move — and 48 orphan vertices on the biped. Found by walking the index buffer and counting which vertices are actually used.
4 A scratch buffer aliased its own input. The curve evaluator wrote its result into a caller buffer that was one of the curve's control points, so it silently rewrote each limb's joint positions as it tessellated them. Mirrored limbs ended up with degenerate joint arcs, hence zero-length blend windows. Alternating tubes with 0 non-zero weights; every second limb unskinned.
5 The superellipsoid bound was inverted. The radial scale factor returned the reciprocal of the correct value, so every lump was scaled outward past its declared radii and the sharp shapes blew up: a 5 cm blob's vertices landed 1.9 units from its own centre. Once corrected, the characters were suddenly the size their presets actually declared — thinner and smaller than the accidental inflated version. A biped 3.86 units tall instead of 2.02, with 66 stray vertices skinned to the neck. Found by logging every emitted vertex whose |y| exceeded 2.
6 The implicit field used for lump normals was identically zero on the surface. The gradient was taken by differencing shapeFactor - 1, which is exactly 0 everywhere on the surface, so every normal came out 0/0 and defaulted to (1,0,0). Nothing in the mesh checks: the surface was closed, correctly oriented, and its face normals "agreed with the vertex normals" 100% — because accumulateNormals rebuilds them from the faces afterwards. The mesh volume was the tell: it had collapsed to a fraction of its bounding box.
7 The tier dial selected a normal nothing used. The vertex shader computed a flat normal into a varying and then lit the smooth one, so the entire "raw primitives" arm of the seam A/B was a no-op. The two normals differ by 35° on average and by more than 10° on 2,740 of 2,938 vertices. Toggling the tier changed 0.3% of pixels, all of it antialiasing. Once the dial drove the lit normal it changed 24%.
8 Orientation was inverted. Two earlier versions of the winding test computed a signed volume relative to a fixed reference point (first the world origin, then the shape's centroid). A relative volume is only an orientation test for a surface that encloses the reference point, which no limb does — so both reported "correct" while half the body was inside out. outwardSum (= Σ centre·normal, reference-free) was negative: −24.2 against a volume of +4.03.
9 The inverse bind pose was the identity. With B⁻¹ = I the skinning matrix degenerates to a rotation about the world origin instead of the bone's own joint, so every articulation tore — and each bone looked correct in isolation. Rest-pose skinning error 36.9 units on a 1.8 m character.
10 Weight indices were not global, and the buffers were never cleared. Inside a shape loop the counter is a local vertex index; using it as a global one made each shape overwrite the weights of the shapes before it. Separately, the pre-sized weight arrays kept the previous creature's values between generations. A hexapod leg skinned to the biped's arm; a whole limb claimed by pelvis.
11 The neck bone skinned nothing, in all five presets. The bone exists and is animated — the head counter-rotates through it — so the rig looked complete while that articulation did nothing. A "every bone skins geometry" check, once it existed.
12 The hop snapped. The launch phase is 0.12 of a cycle and swings the knee's target through 1.8 rad, which is 61.6° in a single 1/30 s frame. Because the hopper's knee-to-ankle bone is short, it also dragged the surface 19× its rest edge length. Measuring per-bone rotation between frames rather than per-frame joint translation (which was small and looked fine).
13 A "does DQS beat LBS" gate whose two sides returned the same number. The dual-quaternion mirror reproduced the LBS figure to four decimal places, so the assertion could never fail. The seam metric had the same shape of error: both of its arms read the same geometry attribute. Reading the reported values instead of the pass/fail column. The unsupportable claims were removed rather than tuned. (With the FK bug fixed the mirror now does separate: LBS 1.21–1.86× against DQS 1.20–1.79×, better on four of five presets — a small, real, and now-reportable difference.)
14 The screenshot gate was measuring a bounding box, not the screen. W1 asserted a "subject fraction" computed by projecting the mesh, and a colour count, and both passed while the biped occupied 34 × 81 pixels in a 1440 × 860 frame — the shot was the far-away all-five overview because moveAll was still on and the characters walked apart every frame, re-widening the camera. Printing an ASCII occupancy map of the screenshots. A gate that measures intent rather than pixels will pass on a frame a person cannot read.
15 The projection helper mirrored Y. project() converted NDC to pixels with the same expression for both axes, but NDC y = +1 is the top of the screen. Every reported box was flipped vertically, so a subject clipped at the bottom read as comfortably in frame. Comparing the reported box against the drawn pixels — a 225×559 px claim against a 115×117 px reality.
16 The "per-primitive" debug view painted three colours. It hashed the part index (body / limbs / detail), so the whole creature came out one flat shade — a documented feature that did not do what its own label said. Turning it on and looking. It now hashes a unique per-shape id (28 ids on the biped, 43 on the hexapod).
17 A "bulk" dial inflated the torsos over the limbs. The per-preset radii are base sizes that the limb attachment points are laid out against — the biped's shoulder sits at x = 0.20 and its chest blob has radius 0.20. Raising the global blob bulk to 1.9 to make the creatures "chunkier" buried every arm and leg inside the torso, so all five rendered as smooth stones. Looking at the overview: five identical rounded lumps. Reverted to 1.0, which is what the preset geometry was actually authored against.
The pattern, and the correction to it. Every one of these produced something that rendered, moved, and passed a screenshot check. None was findable by looking at a still image of the first version, and the browser's console stayed silent throughout. The four numeric checks that found most of them, and that are ~150 lines of test code worth keeping: (a) a rest-pose assertion; (b) max edge stretch over an animation cycle; (c) does every bone skin geometry, and is every vertex referenced by a triangle; (d) watertightness plus a reference-free orientation sum.

But the single worst defect — the one that made every creature render as a featureless lump — was invisible to all sixteen other checks and was found only by looking at the picture. This page originally argued that quality had to be judged numerically because the authoring model could not see images. That was half right: the numbers found subtle structural faults that no eye would catch, and the eye found a catastrophic visual one that no number was asking about. Both halves were needed, and three of the last five defects here were "the verification was measuring the wrong thing" rather than "the code was wrong". A gate is only as good as its question.

Cost model (reasoned, not measured on a device)

  • Draw calls: 1 per character, plus 1 for the outline hull. Five characters ≈ 10.
  • Shader programs: 1 — the outline is the same program with a different uniform.
  • Textures: none. Screen-space passes: none.
  • Skinning: 4 bone indices (one Uint16×4) + 4 weights (Float32×4) per vertex, 4 mat4 fetches, one weighted sum. No bone texture. The optional DQS path adds ~100 ALU.
  • Fragment: one dot product, one quantisation, one rim term. No noise, no fetch.

11. What was not proven

  • No device measurement. Everything above is a software rasteriser on desktop. The frame times the harness reports say what the page costs on SwiftShader, not what a phone GPU would do.
  • No general IK. Knees and elbows are posed analytically; there is no solver and no multi-joint IK chain. Foot lock is exact in the gait frame, not solved in world space.
  • No ragdoll physics. "Ragdoll-like" here describes the look — loose, primitive-composed joints. There is no rigid-body simulation.
  • LOD is not implemented. The tessellation budget is a dial, not an automatic distance LOD.
  • The implicit tier's bake is not characterised on mobile. Its cost is reported per character in the demo's stats panel, but no device budget was measured.
  • Aesthetics were judged by one non-human reviewer. The renders were inspected and the defects above were found that way, which is far more than the numeric gates managed — but "does it look good" is still a human call, and the art direction here (chunky, big-headed, black-outlined toon) has not been reviewed by anyone.
  • Z-fighting where detail primitives sit on a surface. Visible in the per-primitive debug view as speckle on the head and thighs, where an eye or a joint ball is nearly coincident with the blob under it. Invisible in normal shading because both primitives share a colour, but it is there.

12. If this were ported to the game

  1. Take the recipe, not the code. Envelope tubes plus arc-length joint weights plus borrowed weights for joint lumps is ~200 lines of GDScript against a Godot ArrayMesh and a ShaderMaterial; the surrounding JS is scaffolding for the demo.
  2. Take the gates first. The four checks in §9 (rest-pose identity, edge stretch, every-bone- skins-geometry, watertightness + orientation sum) would have caught all six defects, and they are cheap. They belong in the harness next to the existing battery arms.
  3. Keep the tier structure. Having hard / smooth / remesh reachable at runtime is what turned "which is better" into a measurable question. A port should keep the A/B switch even if only one tier ships.
  4. Do not port the DQS path unless weights are known to be bad — measured, it buys nothing once the weights are right.
  5. The style question is separable from the technique. This prototype chose chunky, rounded, black-outlined toon creatures. The same seam machinery would carry a different shape language unchanged.
